Points and distances

Places on the earth in a collection: the metres between two, every row within a radius or a box, the nearest first — what an app adds Redis's GEOADD and GEOSEARCH or PostGIS for.

A geo field holds a point, [lon, lat] in degrees. distance(loc, p) is the metres between two points, within(loc, [west, south, east, north]) whether one lies in a box, and near loc p puts rows in order nearest first. @geo indexes the field, so a radius, a box and the nearest read the rows near the point rather than every row. Every answer is the one a scan of every row gives, row for row.

Every FenecQL block on this page is run, statement by statement, by a test (crates/fenec-core/tests/geo_docs.rs), and each -- → x is the answer it checks: a write's count, or the first value of the first row it answered. The costs are make geo-bench on an M1 Air, over a million points.

Points

A point is written as a list of two numbers, its longitude first — GeoJSON's order, and the order Redis and PostGIS take them in.

create collection places (name text, kind text @hash, loc geo @geo)
put places [{name: "Palermo", kind: "city", loc: [13.361389, 38.115556]}, {name: "Catania", kind: "city", loc: [15.087269, 37.502669]}]   -- → 2
get places select loc where name = "Catania"   -- → [15.087269,37.502669]
  • Redis: GEOADD Sicily 13.361389 38.115556 "Palermo" 15.087269 37.502669 "Catania".
  • Kept as written. The two numbers are f64s and come back as they went in. Redis keeps a 52-bit geohash, which moves a point to the centre of a cell 0.6 m by 0.3 m at the equator: its GEOPOS answers 15.08726745843887329 for the 15.087269 it was given.
  • A longitude from -180 to 180 and a latitude from -90 to 90, or the write is refused: a latitude of 91 is most often the two given the other way round. Redis takes latitudes to 85.05 only.
  • null is no point. A row without one is in no radius and no box, and near answers only rows that hold one.

Distance

distance(a, b) is the metres between two points, on a sphere of Redis's radius (6 372 797.56 m) — so it is GEODIST's answer.

get places select distance(loc, [15.087269, 37.502669]) as m where name = "Palermo"   -- → 166274.2577914073
  • Redis: GEODIST Sicily Palermo Catania answers 166274.1516, between the points it keeps: 0.11 m off, since it moved each to its cell. Over 2 000 pairs of points, crowded and spread over the earth, the two agree to 0.05 mm from the points Redis keeps (GEOPOS), and to 0.54 m from the points as written.
  • PostGIS: ST_Distance(a, b, false) over geography is on a sphere of the earth's mean radius, 6 371 008.8 m, so its metres are 0.03% fewer: Palermo to Catania is 166 227.6 m. Its default, the WGS84 ellipsoid, differs from a sphere by up to 0.5%: 166 506.2 m.
  • The same bits everywhere. The trigonometry is fenecdb's own, not the platform's: a point on a radius's edge is in or out alike on a server, a phone and a page.

Within a radius

get places select name where distance(loc, [15, 37]) <= 200000   -- → "Palermo": in id order
get places select name where distance(loc, [15, 37]) <= 100000   -- → "Catania"
get places where distance(loc, [15, 37]) <= 200000 count         -- → 2
  • Redis: GEOSEARCH Sicily FROMLONLAT 15 37 BYRADIUS 200 km.
  • A radius reaches the index written distance(loc, p) <= r or < r, either way round, in an and chain, the point and the radius each a literal or a parameter. A ring — distance(loc, p) > 1000 beside it — is tested on the rows the radius found.
  • Any radius: 0 is the point itself, and one past half the earth's circumference is every row. A radius over a pole takes every longitude, and one across the antimeridian both sides of it.

Nearest first

near loc p orders the rows by their distance from p, a tie by id, and _score is the distance in metres:

get places select name, distance(loc, [15, 37]) as m near loc [15, 37] limit 1   -- → "Catania"
get places select name where distance(loc, [15, 37]) <= 200000 near loc [15, 37] limit 10   -- → "Catania": then Palermo
  • Redis: GEOSEARCH Sicily FROMLONLAT 15 37 BYRADIUS 200 km ASC COUNT 10 WITHDIST. Redis needs a radius to search in; near needs none.
  • The index is walked nearest first and stops at the page: the ten nearest of a million points took 33 to 38 us, whether they were a street or an ocean away.
  • _score is an f32, as every score is: to the metre out to 16 777 km, and 2 m past it. Select distance(loc, p) for the f64.
  • near loc p exact measures every row, as the collection without the index does: the same answer.

In a box

within(loc, [west, south, east, north]) holds a point inside the box or on its edge. A box whose west is east of its east crosses the antimeridian:

get places where within(loc, [12, 37, 16, 39]) count   -- → 2
put places [{name: "Suva", kind: "city", loc: [178.4419, -18.1248]}, {name: "Apia", kind: "city", loc: [-171.7513, -13.8333]}, {name: "Nuku'alofa", kind: "city", loc: [-175.2018, -21.1394]}]   -- → 3
get places where within(loc, [170, -25, -170, -10]) count   -- → 3
get places select name near loc [180, -15] limit 1   -- → "Suva"
  • Redis's BYBOX is a width and a height in metres around a centre; within takes the corners, as a map's visible area gives them. The rows of a map's view are within(loc, $bounds) with limit.
  • PostGIS: loc && ST_MakeEnvelope(w, s, e, n, 4326), whose box does not wrap: one across the antimeridian is two.

With the rest of a query

A radius or a box is one condition of an and chain, so the rest of the query goes with it: other conditions, an order, a count, a facet, a lookup.

put places [{name: "Cafe A", kind: "cafe", loc: [15.0912, 37.5047]}, {name: "Cafe B", kind: "cafe", loc: [15.0855, 37.5076]}, {name: "Fish market", kind: "market", loc: [15.0868, 37.5005]}]   -- → 3
get places select name where kind = "cafe" and distance(loc, [15.087269, 37.502669]) <= 1000 near loc [15.087269, 37.502669] limit 5   -- → "Cafe A"
get places where kind = "cafe" and distance(loc, [15.087269, 37.502669]) <= 1000 count   -- → 2
get places select name where distance(loc, [15.087269, 37.502669]) <= 1000 facet kind
  • Whichever index names fewer rows is read, and the other condition tested on each: a @hash bucket of a few dozen cafes, or the rows of a radius narrower than the bucket. A near over a set of a few thousand rows measures it whole rather than walk the points.
  • A scoped token finds only the points its rules let it read: its filter is ANDed in as it is into every statement (Security).
  • or reads every row, as it does for any index. Two radii are two statements, or one statement each in a /batch.

Positions that expire

Couriers, drivers, a user's last known place: each one's position written over as it moves, and gone when it stops being sent. @ttl takes a row out of every read once its time is past.

create collection couriers (name text @unique, loc geo @geo, seen timestamp @ttl(30s))
put couriers {name: "ada", loc: [15.0901, 37.5031], seen: now()} if absent else set {loc: new.loc, seen: new.seen}   -- → 1
put couriers {name: "bo", loc: [15.0702, 37.5120], seen: now()} if absent else set {loc: new.loc, seen: new.seen}    -- → 1
get couriers select name near loc [15.087269, 37.502669] limit 1   -- → "ada"
-- 20 s later
put couriers {name: "bo", loc: [15.0880, 37.5030], seen: now()} if absent else set {loc: new.loc, seen: new.seen}    -- → 1: bo moved
get couriers select name near loc [15.087269, 37.502669] limit 1   -- → "bo"
-- 20 s later
get couriers select name near loc [15.0901, 37.5031] limit 1       -- → "bo": ada's position is 40 s old
get couriers count   -- → 1
  • An upsert a position: the row made the first time, its point and time set after, one statement whichever it is.
  • Gone at once: a row past its @ttl is out of every read the moment it is, and the server's sweeper deletes it within the minute after (Rows that expire).

From a client

Over HTTP a point is a JSON array, and a filter is FenecQL's:

curl -X POST http://127.0.0.1:8080/places -d '{"name": "Catania", "loc": [15.087269, 37.502669]}'
curl 'http://127.0.0.1:8080/places?select=name&where=distance(loc,%5B15,37%5D)%3C%3D200000'
curl -X POST http://127.0.0.1:8080/places/near -d '{"field": "loc", "vector": [15, 37], "limit": 10}'
curl -X POST http://127.0.0.1:8080/query \
  -d '{"query": "get places select name near loc $1 limit 10", "params": [[15, 37]]}'

The builders write it as an object condition:

import { connect, distance, type Point } from '@fenecdb/web/client';
import type { FenecSchema } from './fenec-schema.js';   // fenec types app.fenec

const db = connect<FenecSchema>('http://127.0.0.1:8080');
const here: Point = [15.087269, 37.502669];
const cafes = await db.from('places')
  .select('name', distance('loc', here).as('m'))
  .where({ kind: 'cafe', loc: { distance: { from: here, lte: 1000 } } })
  .near('loc', here)
  .limit(10)
  .rows();
const inView = await db.from('places').where({ loc: { within: [12, 37, 16, 39] } }).rows();

Python's, Go's, .NET's, Swift's, Kotlin's and Dart's builders take the same within and distance conditions and render the same text. fenec types writes a geo field as each language's pair of doubles, and a Drizzle table's geometry('loc', { type: 'point' }) with a GiST index is a geo field with @geo.

  • The degrees as written. Every reader without a schema — a query's parameters, a JSON body, the browser module — reads a list of numbers as a vector's f32s, which would move a point by up to 1.7 m at the antimeridian. A list going to a geo field or a distance, within or near over one is read again as written.
  • In the page. The browser module answers the same statements over a database in the page or a synced replica, its index and all.

From PostGIS

fenec import takes a geometry(Point, 4326) or geography(Point) column, or PostgreSQL's own point, as a geo field:

fenec import postgres://app@db:5432/app --table stores --into stores --index "loc@geo"

A column of other shapes — lines, polygons — or of another SRID is refused naming it; --follow mirrors the table as it changes (Import).

What it costs

A million points crowded round 48 cities, as a shop's or a courier's lie, make geo-bench: fenecdb in process; PostGIS 3.5 (a geography(Point) column, GiST, ST_DWithin(…, false) and <->) and Redis 7 (GEOSEARCH) in Docker, each server's own mean time of the query from pg_stat_statements, planning included, and INFO commandstats — the network's 0.3 ms left out.   • A put with @geo costs 1.19 us, against 0.62 without an index and 0.98 with @sorted on a float.
  • Spread evenly over the earth, a million points are about 20 km apart: a radius of up to 10 km took 5 to 10 us, 100 km 24 us, the ten nearest 35 us.
  • Redis sorts every member of the cells round the point by distance: the ten nearest anywhere is a search past half the earth, every member measured. PostGIS plans each statement again, half a millisecond of its time, and its GiST over geography read 132 pages for a radius of 100 m.
  • Over HTTP a request adds its round trip, as it does to PostgreSQL's and Redis's: 0.3 ms through Docker's network here.

What this does not cover

  • Shapes other than points: no lines, polygons or a point in a polygon. A box is the one area a query takes; a polygon's bounding box narrows, and the app tests the rest.
  • The ellipsoid. Distances are on the sphere, as Redis's are: within 0.5% of WGS84's, which PostGIS's geography takes by default.
  • A distance between two rows in one statement: the other row's point is read first and passed as a parameter.
  • GEOHASH strings and STORE: a search's rows are answered, not written to another key.
  • order distance(...): an order takes fields, so the nearest first is near.
