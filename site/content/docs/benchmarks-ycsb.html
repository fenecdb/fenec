  • Durable writes from many threads share their fsyncs. At 16 threads fenecdb's lock is released before the fsync, so one fsync covers every write that landed meanwhile; SQLite syncs each commit under its write lock and stays at one thread's rate.
  • Durable over the wire is compared inside Docker. On a Mac, PostgreSQL and MongoDB run in Docker's Linux virtual machine, whose fsync took 0.08 to 0.10 ms where the Mac's F_FULLFSYNC took 3.9 ms, so the durable tables put fenec-server in the same machine; the server on the Mac is beside them for what a real flush to the disk costs it.
  • Buffered over the wire is mostly the network. Docker's network costs a request about a quarter of a millisecond: the emptiest request took 0.334 ms to PostgreSQL and 0.342 ms to MongoDB at the median, 0.281 ms to fenec-server in Docker and 0.019 ms to it on the Mac. The rows fenec-server in Docker as well are the like-for-like there: ahead of PostgreSQL at one client in A, B, D and E, behind in C and F by 2% and 1%; ahead in every workload at 4 and 16 clients; and ahead of MongoDB in every cell.

Where fenecdb loses, and why.

  • Nowhere in process. fenecdb is ahead of SQLite in every cell; the closest are C at four threads, 1.43 M against 1.33 M, and E durable at one, 2.94 k against 2.81 k. It lost the buffered reads after the updates until its file compacted on its own (C at one thread 30.1 k against 279 k, now 438 k; below).
  • fenec-server in Docker against PostgreSQL, at one client: buffered C 3.07 k against 3.14 k and F 2.07 k against 2.09 k. A read is a round trip of about 0.29 ms, and taken apart 0.25 ms of it is Docker's port forwarding, the same for both; fenec-server's own part is 0.018 ms, PostgreSQL's bind and execute 0.016, and the answer the same size, 1.3 KB against 1.2 in one packet. The rest moves with the VM rather than with either server, by more than the gap from one day to the next: PostgreSQL's C at one client did 3.66 k on 2026-10-05, 3.14 k here, fenec-server's 3.19 k and 3.07 k. E was the gap that was fenec-server's: a scan of 50 rows spent 213-220 µs inside it, half decoding each row into values and half writing them out, and took 37 page faults, musl giving the answer's memory back to the system and faulting it in again. Written straight from the stored rows into a buffer the connection keeps, it takes 50-60 µs and none: on 2026-10-05 E went 1.03 k -> 1.62 k buffered and 1.06 k -> 1.82 k durable, and here it does 1.49 k against PostgreSQL's 1.26 k buffered, 1.69 k against 1.28 k durable. Durable A and F, where it was behind, are ahead too: an fsync of a file that grew commits ext4's journal, 0.36 ms against 0.065 in place, and a durable write syncs a log of fixed size beside the file instead (A 2.24 k -> 3.27 k on 2026-10-04; here 3.34 k against PostgreSQL's 2.52 k).
  • fenec-server on the Mac, durable A and F: A 0.53 k at one client and 3.94 k at 16, F 0.49 k and 4.05 k. Half the operations are writes, and each waits for F_FULLFSYNC, 3.9 ms on this disk; it is beside the Docker columns, not against them.

Beside Redis

Redis 7.4, the official redis:7 image, in a container of its own in the systems' turns, asked over redis-rs 1.7.1 by a connection a client thread, in the same cells as fenec-server in Docker. It is another kind of system from the rest, and the tables read with that in mind:

  • Everything is in memory. Redis holds the whole data set in its memory and answers from it; nothing is read from disk, and a data set larger than the memory is not an option. fenec-server reads its file through the operating system's page cache, PostgreSQL and MongoDB through their buffers. What each held is the last table below, and what it means for a cache in front of fenec-server is on Instead of Redis.
  • One thread runs the commands. Every command runs on Redis's main thread, one after another, however many clients there are (io-threads is 1 as it comes, and raised it threads the sockets' reading and writing, not the commands). fenec-server reads on a thread a connection under a shared lock and writes under an exclusive one.
  • Its durability is a setting of its own, and each mode is Redis's own terms for it. durable is the append-only file with appendfsync always: the writes are in the file and fsynced before the answers go out, one fsync for the writes of every client that landed meanwhile. buffered is appendfsync everysec, the policy Redis suggests and its default once the file is on: a thread fsyncs once a second, and a crash loses up to a second of answered writes, two if the disk is slow, where fenec-server's --sync 250 loses 250 ms. snapshot is the image as it comes: no append-only file, a snapshot of the data set after 3 600 s and a write, 300 s and 100 writes, or 60 s and 10 000, so a crash loses every write since the last one, minutes of them. Nothing else here is measured that way: it is a column beside the others, not against them. The snapshots stay on in the other two modes, as Redis's persistence page advises both "if you want a degree of data safety comparable to what PostgreSQL can provide you"; each snapshot and each rewrite of the append-only file is a fork, and its copied pages and its writing fall inside the cells.
  • A record is a hash, and a scan needs an index of its own. Each is as YCSB's own Redis binding has it (RedisClient.java): a hash a record, read whole by HGETALL, an update an HSET of its one field, and every key a member of a sorted set, _indices, from which a scan takes its keys (ZRANGEBYSCORE) before it reads their records. Three things differ, each in Redis's favour or neither's: a key's score is the key, where the binding scatters keys by a hash of their text, so a scan reads keys in order from its own as every other system's does; an insert's HSET and ZADD are one MULTI/EXEC, one round trip and one fsync, where the binding sends them apart; and a scan's records come in one pipeline, where the binding asks for them one at a time, so a scan of 50 records is two round trips rather than 51.
  • Both are behind Docker's network, about a quarter of a millisecond a request, most of a read by key: fenec-server in Docker is the like-for-like, and the server on the Mac is not set against Redis in a container.

What each holds. On disk and in memory after A, B, C, F and D: the memory is the container's own, its processes' anonymous memory and shared memory from its cgroup, the page cache of its files apart, and Redis's is read with no snapshot or rewrite running. fenec-server's file is in the page cache, not in this column, as PostgreSQL's and MongoDB's files beyond their buffers are; Redis's data set is in it whole.

Method and details

YCSB's core workloads, written in the bench crate (crates/fenec-bench/src/bin/ycsb.rs) to YCSB's own definitions rather than run through its Java client: a record is a key and ten fields of 100 random characters, a read reads the whole record, an update writes one field, keys are drawn by YCSB's scrambled zipfian (0.99, hashed over ten billion items) or, for D, its skewed-latest, and a scan reads 1 to 100 records from a key. The key is an integer from 1 (insertorder=ordered without the user prefix), so each system keys by what it keys best by: fenecdb's id, SQLite's INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, a bigint primary key, MongoDB's _id; E's scan is get usertable where id >= $1 limit n, which walks the id index from the key. A read never asks for a key whose insert has not been answered. The sequence is YCSB's: a load, then A, B, C, F and D over that data, then a load afresh for E, at 1, 4 and 16 threads, each cell 30 s after a 5 s warm-up. Latencies go into a log-linear histogram of 64 steps an octave.

The systems. fenecdb 0.1.9 in process, reads under the shared lock and writes under the exclusive one as the server takes them, against SQLite 3.53.2 through rusqlite, a connection a thread, WAL, the file mapped. fenec-server natively, each client thread a kept-alive HTTP connection sending POST /query with parameters, against PostgreSQL 17 (shared_buffers 1 GB, prepared statements) and MongoDB 8 (the official Rust driver), each in a container started for its turn and removed with its volume after, so the virtual machine (8 cores, 3.8 GB) holds one at a time. fenec-server in Docker is the same server in a container of its own image. Durable is an fsync a write in each engine's own terms: a flush and its fsync (F_FULLFSYNC on macOS) and --sync always, synchronous=FULL with fullfsync, synchronous_commit=on, j: true; buffered is each one's default: a flush every 250 ms (--sync 250), synchronous=NORMAL, synchronous_commit=off, w: 1, j: false. C writes nothing and runs once, buffered. Docker's fsync is the Linux VM's, which does not flush the Mac's disk as F_FULLFSYNC does: a 1 KB append and its F_FULLFSYNC took 3.9 ms on the Mac and its plain fsync 0.023 ms, while in the VM, on a volume as the databases' data, pg_test_fsync gave 0.076 ms for fdatasync and 0.103 for fsync (crates/fenec-bench/ycsb/fsync.txt). So the server is measured in Docker as well, at 1, 4 and 16 threads, durable and buffered, beside it on the Mac.

Why fenecdb's reads slowed down after the updates, and no longer do. An update writes the whole record again at the file's end, and the version it replaces stays dead until a compact. The first three runs (r1-r3) were measured before anything compacted on its own: after A's and B's updates the buffered file had grown to 4.7 to 6.5 GB on this 8 GB machine, against SQLite's 2.1 to 2.5 GB updated in place, and a profile of C at one thread (crates/fenec-bench/ycsb/profile-c1.txt) put 86% of the reading thread's time on the first byte of the record in the mapped file, waiting for its page: about one read in five went to the disk. C at one thread read 30.1 k a second, where the same cell over a file just loaded read 443 k. fenecdb now compacts a file beside its queries once half of it is dead (Compaction), in an app's process as in the server, and the harness runs it as an app would get it: the in-process database with the engine's Compactor, the server with its default --auto-compact. Runs c1-c3 measured fenecdb and fenec-server again with it; the buffered file stood at 3.7 to 4.0 GB after D, mid-way between compacts of a file A's updates grow by 100 MB a second, and C at one thread read 438 k. The earlier runs of fenecdb and fenec-server stay in the results, marked superseded (crates/fenec-bench/ycsb/superseded.tsv); SQLite was not measured again, and the servers were measured again since, all of them on one day (below).

And once the file nears what the page cache can hold. Since these runs a file is also compacted as it nears the memory the system has for its pages, or once the process waits for the disk (Compaction); the cells above predate it. Measured apart on 2026-10-11 and 12, in process, buffered, A, B and F at 1 and 16 threads, the build before it and the one with it in turns, its cells in none of the medians: with the machine's memory as it stood, two runs each, A at 16 threads went from 82 and 92 k to 133 k twice and the rest stayed within the runs' spread; beside 1 GB of memory held by another program, one run each, B went from 23.5 k to 63.5 k at one thread and from 62.3 k to 112.8 k at 16, F from 23.6 k to 48.5 k at 16, the file at its largest 1.2 to 1.5 GB against 1.5 to 2.0, and A at one thread from 29.7 k to 24.1 k.

Every server on one day. On the night of 2026-10-13, fenec-server natively and in Docker, PostgreSQL, MongoDB and Redis measured every cell, A to F at 1, 4 and 16 clients, durable and buffered, three times, in turns with three idle minutes between (runs rx1-rx3, 8.5 hours). The machine. A fanless M1 MacBook Air, 8 GB, macOS, which slows to a third under minutes of load on every core: each cell starts once a one-core probe runs within 4% of its idle time, the probe's ratio written beside the cell, and the systems take turns with three idle minutes between them.

The answers are checked. --verify logs every operation and what it was answered, and after each cell holds each one to what was written: a read must answer the record it asked for, each field holding the value of a write that could have been the last one before it (one that ended before the read began and no other ended after it, or one that overlapped the read); a scan must answer consecutive keys from its own, as many as it asked for wherever they existed, each record as written; a write must report one record written. Every write is logged, not a sample, because judging a read under concurrent writes takes every write's span; the log is a push into each thread's own list and the check runs once the cell is over, so nothing is compared in an operation's time. The same check holds every system. A pass over all six — 100 000 records, 10 s a cell, workloads A, E and F at 1 and 16 threads, durable and buffered — checked 13.8 million reads, 2.7 million scans of 134.7 million rows and 9.8 million writes and found no mismatch (crates/fenec-bench/ycsb/verify.tsv); its timings are not published.
