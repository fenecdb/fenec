Replication

A primary sends its replicas the writes that have reached its disk, and each applies them to a file of its own and serves reads from it. A replica refuses writes until it is promoted, by hand; what a failover can lose is what the primary's sync policy had not yet put on disk.

# the primary: replicas are fed over its HTTP listener
fenec-pg --file primary.fenec --http 0.0.0.0:8080 --http-token "$HTTP" \
         --replication-token "$REPL" --sync always

# a replica, over a file of its own
fenec-pg --file replica.fenec --listen 0.0.0.0:5433 --http 0.0.0.0:8081 \
         --replication-token "$REPL" --replica-of http://primary:8080

# the primary is gone: the replica takes writes from here on
curl -X POST replica:8081/_replication/promote -H "Authorization: Bearer $REPL"
# {"promoted":true,"seq":41873,"history":"5f0e2c9a81b44d17"}

A replica is a whole fenec-pg: every read works on it over PostgreSQL and HTTP, subscriptions included, from its own copy in memory. A write gets 25006 over PostgreSQL, the code a PostgreSQL standby answers with, and 403 over HTTP; SHOW transaction_read_only says on and pg_is_in_recovery() says true, which is how libpq's target_session_attrs and JDBC's targetServerType tell it from the primary.

What is sent

A fenecdb file is already a log: an image, then every write appended as a record. A replica is sent those records, whole and in order, and applies each the way the write path made it — the document, and the upkeep of every index on it — and appends it to its own file. Its file therefore ends as the primary's does, and a replica stopped and started again reopens where it was and asks only for what came after. The primary keeps the last 64 MiB of writes for this (--replication-buffer); a replica further behind than that is sent an image of the whole database instead, and follows write by write from there.

Only writes on the primary's disk are sent. A write enters the feed when it is appended and leaves it once an fsync has covered it, so a primary that crashes comes back holding every write any replica was sent, and a replica never has to be told to forget one. The price is latency, set by the primary's --sync: under always a write goes out right after its own fsync, under 250 up to 250 ms later. Under off nothing is on disk before shutdown, so a server with a replication token refuses to start with it.

Positions and histories

Every write moves the change counter by one, on the primary and on each replica alike, so a replica's position is its own counter and a subscriber's cursor means the same change on either. A block — the writes of one statement, a /batch or a transaction — is one record numbered by its last write, and is sent and applied whole: a replica never stands inside one. A replica of a replica is fed exactly as its primary was.

Numbers alone stop being enough once a history forks. A replica promoted after its primary died writes its own change 1 001; the old primary, or a second replica that had got further along, may hold a different 1 001. Streaming the new primary's 1 002 on top of that would go unnoticed. So each fork gets a fresh id and remembers the change where it left its parent, as PostgreSQL numbers its timelines, and a replica is continued only from a position the primary's own history passed through:

The history lives in the file (record kind 8), and the first time a primary serves replicas it draws an id of its own — the root history is every database's, so it names none.

Promotion

By hand, one of two ways: POST /_replication/promote on the replica's HTTP listener, or restarting it over its file with --promote in place of --replica-of. Either forks the history where the replica stands and turns writes on. A replica's file does not open any other way — started with neither flag, fenec-pg refuses and says which to give — because a replica taking writes without a fork would continue its primary's history with writes the primary never made.

Making sure the old primary takes no more writes is the operator's part, as it is with PostgreSQL: nothing here fences it. Brought back, it rejoins under the new primary with --replica-of, and is sent an image if it wrote past the fork.

What a failover loses

A primary killed with SIGKILL while one client writes to it as fast as it answers, the replica promoted half a second later, ten times per policy; counted are the writes the client was told succeeded that the promoted replica does not hold (make replica-bench, macOS, loopback):

primary's --syncacknowledged writes/slost per failover
always2840 in all ten
25053 5659 700–10 788: the last ≤ 201 ms

Under 250 the loss is the policy's own: those writes were answered before any fsync and were not on the primary's disk either. Under always a write is answered after its fsync and sent right after, so what can be lost is a write whose answer went out in the fraction of a millisecond before it was sent — none of the ten failovers caught one. No failover left the replica holding a write the client had not been answered for.

What it costs

Primary and replica in one process over loopback, each over its own file; lag is from a write's answer on the primary to the write being readable on the replica, over 1 000 writes:

A replica keeps up with a bulk load: 100 000 documents of 128 dimensions with an HNSW index, which the primary wrote in 23.4 s in statements of 1 000, were applied from the stream in 19.7 s — 5 088 writes a second, the graph built as they arrive. A new replica of that database sent it as an image — 58.3 MB, the graph in it — answered from it 0.17 s after it connected.

The primary keeps the writes in memory up to its buffer, and copies the image it sends; a replica holds the database in memory as a primary does. The replica's HNSW graph is built from the same vectors in the same order, but not in the same batches, so an approximate near can rank a close call the other way on the two; everything else answers identically.

Backups and restoring to a moment

The same stream written to a directory instead of applied is an archive: base images, and every write after them with the time the primary appended it. The fenec command line writes and reads one:

# the running database, taken whole: a file that opens as a database of its own
fenec backup http://primary:8080 nightly.fenec --token "$REPL"

# every write that reaches the primary's disk, until interrupted
fenec archive http://primary:8080 /backups/archive --token "$REPL"

# a base image into the archive -- a day's worth, say -- so a restore replays less
fenec backup http://primary:8080 /backups/archive --token "$REPL"

# the database as it stood just before the mistake
fenec restore /backups/archive before.fenec --to 2026-09-22T10:14:59Z
fenec restore /backups/archive before.fenec --to-change 41873

A restore takes the latest image at or before the point asked for and appends the archived writes after it up to there — a fenecdb file is an image and the writes after it, so that is the whole restore — then opens the result to check it, forks its history and checkpoints it. --to keeps every write the primary appended at or before that moment and stops at the first one after it; --to-change stops after that change. A statement writes each document as a change of its own, so a del of ten documents is ten changes.

The archive is fed as a replica is: only writes that were on the primary's disk, so none a crash took back, and a compact on the primary loses it nothing — the writes were archived before a compact folded them into an image. An archiver that falls further behind than the primary keeps writes for takes a new image and goes on from there; the archive then holds no write between the two images, and a restore to a point in that gap says so rather than inventing one. Restoring the 100 000 × 128 load above from an image and its 100 000 writes took 19.9 s, most of it building the graph for them, and the file then opened in 63 ms; to the load's middle, 7.8 s. A base image a day keeps a restore to a day of writes.

image-<change>-<time>.fenec   a base image: the database at that change
writes-<first change>.log      per record, the time it was appended and the record
history                        the history the archive is on

Pruning is by hand: a restore needs an image at or before its point and every segment after that image, so what is older than the newest image before the earliest moment worth keeping can go.

Endpoints

GET  /_replication?since=<change>&history=<id>   the stream a replica is fed
GET  /_replication/status                          role, position, replicas, lag
POST /_replication/promote                         a replica's promotion

All three want Authorization: Bearer <replication token>, not the --http-token: the token is for replicas and for whoever promotes them. The status of a replica names its primary and says whether it is connected, how many writes it is behind the primary's disk, and how long ago it last heard from it; a primary's lists the replicas it is feeding and how far each has been sent.

Flags

--replication-token <value>turns replication on; needs --file, and --http to feed replicas. Also read from FENEC_REPLICATION_TOKEN
--replica-of http://host:portfollows that primary into --file; needs the token
--promoteopens a replica's file to take writes, forking its history
--replication-buffer <MiB>writes kept for replicas that fall behind; default 64

What it does not do

  • No synchronous replication. A write is answered when it is on the primary's disk, never when a replica has it.
  • No automatic failover of a single file. Promotion is by hand, and so is keeping the old primary from taking writes. A router's tenants can have one: nodes that take its lease stop writing when they lose it, and are failed over after (Automatic failover).
  • No TLS. The stream is plain HTTP, like every other listener here: across an open network, a tunnel carries it.
  • No retention. Nothing prunes an archive's images and segments on its own.
